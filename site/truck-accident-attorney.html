Truck Accident Attorney: 2026 Legal and Claims Guide

Learn how a truck accident attorney investigates commercial crashes, secures black box data, and handles multi-party liability in 2026.

Sarah Mitchell
Recent
18 min read

Collisions involving commercial semi-trucks, tractor-trailers, and large freight carriers represent some of the most catastrophic events on modern highways. When an eighty-thousand-pound commercial motor vehicle collides with a standard passenger car, the resulting physical destruction and bodily injuries are often severe, life-altering, and permanent. In the immediate aftermath of a wreck, injured victims and their families face mounting intensive care hospital bills, complex corporate structures, and aggressive insurance defense teams determined to minimize financial exposure.

Securing an experienced truck accident attorney transforms this uneven dynamic. Unlike standard two-vehicle automobile accidents, commercial trucking collisions involve intricate federal safety statutes, multiple potentially liable corporate entities, and critical digital evidence that can vanish within weeks if not properly preserved. Understanding how specialized commercial truck accident lawyers investigate these wrecks, unearth corporate negligence, navigate complex multi-layered commercial insurance policies, and secure full economic recovery is vital for anyone confronting the aftermath of a catastrophic highway crash.

The Critical Differences Between Truck and Passenger Car Accidents

Many motorists assume that an accident involving an 18-wheeler accident attorney or a commercial tractor-trailer is simply an enlarged car accident claim. In reality, the legal, financial, and mechanical frameworks governing commercial vehicle collisions are entirely distinct. Grasping these differences explains why standard personal injury tactics are insufficient when confronting multi-million-dollar motor carriers and their defense syndicates.

The first fundamental disparity lies in basic vehicular physics. A fully loaded semi-truck can legally weigh up to 80,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) across standard interstate corridors without special overweight permits, whereas the average passenger car weighs between 3,000 and 4,000 pounds. This twenty-fold weight disparity generates tremendous kinetic energy transfer upon impact. Motorists operating passenger vehicles like a Honda Civic Sport or compact crossover absorb virtually all the destructive energy during a big rig collision. The resulting physical trauma includes traumatic brain injury, diffuse axonal injury, cervical spine burst fracture, spinal cord trauma resulting in paraplegia or quadriplegia, internal organ laceration, and traumatic limb amputation.

The second major difference is the aggressive, immediate corporate defense deployed by trucking companies. Large freight corporations and commercial auto insurance syndicates maintain rapid-response investigation teams consisting of independent adjusters, accident reconstruction specialists, and defense attorneys. These teams are frequently dispatched to crash sites within hours of a collision. Their explicit mission is to gather perishable physical evidence, inspect mechanical systems, download engine telematics, interview surviving drivers, and build a defense narrative before injured victims have even left the emergency trauma center.

Finally, the regulatory and insurance environments diverge drastically from standard automotive claims. While ordinary drivers carry basic state-mandated auto policies, interstate motor carriers operate under strict federal safety mandates and carry substantial multi-tier liability policies. Investigating these claims requires deep knowledge of commercial transportation statutes, corporate leasing contracts, and digital vehicle telematics.

The Four-Tier Commercial Liability Matrix

In a standard car accident, civil liability typically rests solely with the at-fault driver. In commercial trucking litigation, however, civil fault is rarely confined to a single individual. Commercial freight operations rely on complex webs of corporate partnerships, third-party logistics brokers, and contracted maintenance providers. A skilled truck accident attorney conducts a multi-tier liability analysis to identify all solvent entities responsible for contributing to the crash.

1. Commercial Motor Carrier (Trucking Company)

The motor carrier operating the vehicle is frequently the primary target of civil liability. Under the common law legal doctrine of respondeat superior, an employer is vicariously liable for the negligent actions performed by an employee within the scope of their employment. If an employed truck driver speeds, makes an illegal turn, or violates federal hours of service regulations, the trucking company shares direct legal responsibility.

Beyond vicarious liability, motor carriers often face direct corporate negligence claims. An attorney examines corporate records to determine whether the company engaged in negligent hiring by retaining a driver with a documented history of reckless driving, suspended commercial driver licenses, or failed drug and alcohol screening tests. Direct claims also encompass negligent supervision, failure to conduct required Department of Transportation inspections, failure to train, and establishing unrealistic delivery deadlines that incentivize drivers to exceed maximum driving limits.

Additionally, motor carriers often attempt to shield themselves from liability by classifying drivers as independent contractors or owner-operators. Under the federal statutory employee doctrine, federal courts and safety regulations view any individual driving a commercial vehicle displaying the carrier USDOT number and MC operating authority as an employee for liability purposes, defeating efforts to escape vicarious responsibility.

2. The Truck Driver

Individual commercial drivers hold a commercial driver license (CDL) with specialized endorsements and are held to higher professional standards than ordinary motorists. Individual driver liability centers on identifiable negligent conduct, such as operating while fatigued, driving under the influence of narcotics or alcohol, texting while driving in violation of federal mobile phone prohibitions, or failing to conduct mandatory pre-trip mechanical inspections. In cases involving gross corporate recklessness, drunk driving, or intentional falsification of logbooks, punitive damages may be pursued directly.

3. Shippers, Freight Brokers, and Cargo Loaders

Freight logistics involves independent third parties who load and secure shipping containers, dry vans, flatbed trailers, and refrigerated units. When cargo is loaded unevenly, exceeds axle gross weight distribution restrictions, or lacks adequate tie-down restraints, the physical dynamics of the truck change dramatically. Shifting freight causes severe trailer sway, trailer jackknife accidents, or fatal rollover wrecks. If a third-party warehouse shipping facility loaded the cargo improperly, they can be held independently liable under aggregate working load limit standards.

Freight brokers who match shipping customers with independent motor carriers also face legal exposure under claims for negligent selection of a carrier. Grounded in the Restatement (Second) of Torts Section 411, if a logistics broker hires an unsafe motor carrier with an unsatisfactory safety rating, suspended operating authority, or a pattern of safety violations without reviewing its Pre-Employment Screening Program (PSP) data or safety compliance scores, the broker can be held liable. While brokers often argue federal preemption under the Federal Aviation Administration Authorization Act (FAAAA / 49 U.S.C. 14501), modern court decisions regularly uphold state law negligence claims concerning highway safety.

4. Maintenance Vendors and Equipment Manufacturers

Commercial trucks log hundreds of thousands of highway miles annually, demanding rigorous, continuous maintenance. Many fleet operators outsource mechanical repairs to third-party maintenance contractors. If a crash occurs due to worn brake linings, improper automatic slack adjusters, or steering linkage failure, the repair facility that signed off on the vehicle inspection may bear direct liability. Similarly, if an axle snaps or a steer tire experiences tread separation or retread tire delamination due to a manufacturing defect, a strict product liability lawsuit may be initiated against the parts manufacturer.

Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations (49 CFR)

Commercial trucking is governed by Title 49 of the Code of Federal Regulations (49 CFR), enforced by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA). Establishing that a driver or trucking enterprise violated these federal safety statutes is one of the most effective strategies for proving liability, establishing negligence per se in civil court.

Federal Regulation Safety Area Governed Critical Legal Relevance in Litigation
49 CFR Part 382 Controlled Substances & Alcohol Testing Mandates pre-employment, random, post-accident drug screens, and reporting to the FMCSA Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse.
49 CFR Part 390 General Applicability & Safety Rules Establishes motor carrier safety recordkeeping, accident registers, and carrier operating authority requirements.
49 CFR Part 391 Driver Qualification Standards Requires driver qualification files, Form MCSA-5875 medical examiner certificates, road tests, and multi-state CDL checks.
49 CFR Part 392 Driving of Commercial Motor Vehicles Prohibits driving while fatigued, mandates reduced speed in adverse weather, and bans handheld mobile devices and radar detectors.
49 CFR Part 393 Parts and Accessories Necessary for Safety Dictates lighting, pneumatic air brake standards, tire load ratings, FMVSS 223/224 rear impact guards, and cargo securement rules.
49 CFR Part 395 Hours of Service (HOS) Regulations Enforces the 11-hour driving limit, 14-hour on-duty window, mandatory 30-minute rest break, and electronic logging device (ELD) data integrity.
49 CFR Part 396 Inspection, Repair, and Maintenance Mandates systematic maintenance schedules, annual DOT safety audits, and daily vehicle inspection reports (DVIR).

Among these regulations, Hours of Service rules under Part 395 play a central role in truck accident litigation. Driver fatigue and drowsy driving remain leading contributing factors in catastrophic highway collisions. Federal rules prohibit property-carrying commercial drivers from driving more than 11 total hours after 10 consecutive hours off duty. Additionally, drivers cannot drive beyond the 14th consecutive hour after coming on duty, and they must take a mandatory 30-minute rest break after 8 cumulative hours of driving. Furthermore, drivers cannot exceed 60 hours on duty in 7 consecutive days, or 70 hours in 8 consecutive days, requiring a mandatory 34-hour restart period before beginning a new cycle.

Attorneys subpoena electronic logging device (ELD) records to expose widespread industry non-compliance. Common tactics to circumvent safety caps include ghost logs, where drivers operate without logging into the telematics terminal, and the fraudulent misuse of personal conveyance mode under 49 CFR 395.8 to continue moving commercial freight while technically logged off-duty. Lawyers cross-reference ELD timestamped data with highway toll transponders, GPS fleet tracking software, Qualcomm satellite messaging, and fuel credit card receipts to prove intentional falsification.

Digital Forensics and the 30-Day Evidence Preservation Window

One of the most urgent reasons to contact a truck accident attorney immediately after a collision is the fragile nature of commercial truck evidence. Unlike physical skid marks that weather away over several weeks, commercial vehicle digital data can disappear permanently in as little as thirty days due to automated digital overwrite cycles.

The Electronic Control Module (ECM) and Event Data Recorder (EDR)

Modern commercial heavy trucks are equipped with specialized computing systems known as Electronic Control Modules (ECM) and Event Data Recorders (EDR), commonly referred to as the truck black box. These units continuously monitor engine performance and record crucial baseline metrics immediately before, during, and after an impact, including:

  • Vehicle travel speed in one-second intervals leading up to the collision
  • Engine revolutions per minute (RPM) and highway speed governor settings
  • Throttle percentage and whether the driver was accelerating into the hazard
  • Pneumatic brake application, air pressure levels, and anti-lock brake system (ABS) activation
  • Steering wheel input angles and abrupt evasive maneuvers
  • Delta-V, measuring the sudden change in velocity and kinetic crush force
  • Seatbelt status, forward collision warning alerts, and airbag deployment timing
  • Forward-facing dash cam footage and dual-facing cabin camera recordings

The Threat of Spoliation and Formal Preservation Letters

Most commercial fleet ECM units operate on continuous looping memory. If the truck remains in service or if the engine is restarted multiple times following a crash, the new operational data overwrites the stored pre-crash records. Furthermore, trucking companies have been known to quickly repair, scrap, or sell damaged vehicles to eliminate physical evidence before an independent inspection can be conducted.

To prevent this destruction, an attorney sends a formal Spoliation Letter, also known as an Evidence Preservation Notice, to the motor carrier, its insurance company, and all affiliated entities. This formal legal document places the recipients on formal notice that civil litigation is pending and explicitly prohibits the alteration, deletion, repair, or destruction of:

  • The tractor and trailer in their post-crash, unaltered physical condition
  • All ECM, EDR, telematics, and dash-cam video recordings
  • Driver Electronic Logging Device records for the preceding six months
  • Driver Qualification Files, medical certificates, and drug test results
  • Annual vehicle inspection reports and work orders for the past twelve months
  • Cellular phone records and Qualcomm onboard dispatch messaging transcripts
  • Bills of lading, cargo weight tickets, and warehouse manifest documents

If a motor carrier fails to preserve this data after receiving notice, trial courts issue severe sanctions against the defense. These include issuing adverse inference jury instructions, which direct jurors to presume that the destroyed evidence would have proven the trucking company negligence, or striking the defense answer entirely to enter judgment on liability.

Mechanical Systems and Forensic Crash Dynamics

Commercial trucking wrecks exhibit unique physical dynamics that require specialized forensic reconstruction. Investigating attorneys work with certified accident reconstruction engineers, mechanical specialists, and commercial motor vehicle inspectors to pinpoint the precise mechanical or behavioral failure that precipitated the disaster.

Pneumatic air brake failures represent a frequent category of mechanical negligence. Heavy semi-trucks rely on compressed air reservoirs, air compressor governors, and S-cam drum brakes rather than hydraulic fluid. Air brakes require precise manual or automatic pushrod stroke adjustments. When maintenance is neglected, drum brakes overheat on downhill descents, causing severe thermal brake fade where the friction coefficient collapses and stopping distance multiplies exponentially. Similarly, steer axle tire blowouts frequently cause immediate loss of vehicle control, drawing parallels to highway hazards where motorists must understand how to change a tire safely during roadside emergencies.

Another lethal dynamic is the underride collision. In underride crashes, a smaller passenger automobile slides beneath the elevated chassis of a commercial trailer from the rear or side. The hood and crumple zones of the passenger car pass beneath the trailer bed, causing the rear frame of the truck to impact the passenger compartment directly at windshield level. While federal rules mandate rear impact guards under FMVSS 223 and FMVSS 224, side underride guards remain less regulated, making underride accidents particularly devastating. Attorneys investigate whether guards met federal structural standards or failed due to severe rust and prior collision damage.

Trailer jackknifing occurs when a semi-truck drive axle locks up while the trailer continues moving forward, swinging out perpendicularly like a pocketknife. This phenomenon is frequently caused by excessive speed on wet roadways, abrupt braking without anti-lock braking engagement, or improperly adjusted brake pushrods. Rollover collisions often stem from unbaffled liquid cargo tankers, where liquid slosh surges laterally inside the tank, shifting the vehicle high center of gravity during normal highway curve navigation.

Commercial Insurance Policies and Financial Recovery

Because commercial trucking wrecks routinely cause catastrophic harm, federal statutes mandate that motor carriers maintain significantly higher liability insurance minimums than ordinary drivers. Knowing how to locate and claim across these multi-layered policies is essential for securing adequate financial restitution.

Freight & Carrier Classification Federal Minimum Liability Typical Industry Coverage Structure
Non-Hazardous General Freight (over 10,000 lbs) $750,000 Minimum Most commercial fleets carry $1,000,000 primary liability plus umbrella excess layers up to $10,000,000.
Hazardous Materials (Oil, Petroleum, Bulk Waste) $1,000,000 Minimum Tanker fleets routinely purchase $2,000,000 to $5,000,000 primary commercial coverage.
Hazardous Substances & High-Risk Cargo $5,000,000 Minimum Carriers of explosives, toxic inhalation hazards, and radioactive freight carry large syndicates.
Commercial Passenger Transport (16+ passengers) $5,000,000 Minimum Intercity bus operators and commercial charters maintain dedicated catastrophic group policies.

A crucial legal instrument in commercial transportation insurance is the MCS-90 Endorsement, mandated under the Motor Carrier Act and 49 U.S.C. 31139. Form MCS-90 is attached to the motor carrier primary policy to protect the public. Under this endorsement, the insurance company is legally obligated to pay any final judgment recovered against the motor carrier for public liability resulting from negligence in the operation of commercial motor vehicles, regardless of whether the specific vehicle or driver was listed on the policy, or if the trucking company violated policy terms. This prevents insurers from evading liability through internal technical exclusions.

In practice, catastrophic injury claims often exceed a trucking company primary policy limits. An attorney accesses additional layers of compensation by examining commercial umbrella policies, excess liability syndicates, and third-party corporate coverage. When evaluating corporate responsibility, attorneys cross-reference standard commercial liability frameworks similar to those found in professional liability insurance policies, ensuring no potential source of financial recovery is overlooked.

Furthermore, when multiple vehicles are involved in a collision, motor carriers may attempt to shift fault onto other drivers to avoid claims. Understanding how insurance carriers structure risk assessments, comparable to how individuals evaluate options when seeking to get insurance quotes, helps plaintiffs anticipate defensive tactics and maintain strong negotiation positions.

Comparative Negligence Rules Across State Jurisdictions

Because interstate commercial trucking routes cross multiple state boundaries, determining which jurisdiction tort laws apply is a vital element of litigation strategy. State rules regarding comparative fault significantly influence settlement valuations:

  • Pure Comparative Negligence (e.g., California, Florida, New York): An injured plaintiff can recover damages regardless of their percentage of fault, though their recovery is reduced proportionately. If a plaintiff is determined to be 20 percent at fault in a million-dollar claim, they recover eight hundred thousand dollars.
  • Modified Comparative Negligence 50 Percent Bar (e.g., Colorado, Georgia): A plaintiff can recover damages only if their fault is 49 percent or less. If fault is equal at 50 percent, recovery is completely barred.
  • Modified Comparative Negligence 51 Percent Bar (e.g., Texas, Illinois, Ohio): A plaintiff can recover as long as their fault does not exceed 50 percent. If fault reaches 51 percent, recovery is completely barred.
  • Pure Contributory Negligence (e.g., North Carolina, Virginia, Maryland, Alabama): If an injured motorist contributed even one percent of negligence to the wreck, all financial recovery is completely barred. In these jurisdictions, establishing complete carrier negligence without minor plaintiff fault is paramount.

Comprehensive Damages Recoverable in Commercial Trucking Claims

The financial recovery obtained in a commercial truck collision is intended to make the injured party whole under civil law. Given the severity of injuries, damages in truck accident cases are calculated using forward-looking medical and actuarial models rather than simple hospital bill totals.

Economic Damages

Economic damages compensate for quantifiable, out-of-pocket financial losses resulting from the incident. Key components include:

  • Past and Future Medical Expenses: Immediate emergency trauma care, air ambulance transport, surgeries, extended intensive care stays, prescription medications, assistive medical devices, and in-home nursing care.
  • Life Care Plans: For permanent disabilities such as quadriplegia or severe cognitive impairment, a certified life care planner assesses lifetime costs, including home architectural accessibility modifications, wheelchair replacement cycles, and specialized physical therapy.
  • Lost Wages and Diminished Earning Capacity: Direct compensation for income lost during medical recovery, as well as actuarial calculations of future income lost if the victim can never return to their profession or full-time employment.
  • Property Damage: Full replacement value of the destroyed passenger vehicle and any personal items lost in the wreck. When settling total vehicle losses, adjusters analyze market valuation standards seen across used cars for sale to substantiate fair market cash settlements.

Non-Economic Damages

Non-economic damages address the intangible, human consequences of severe physical trauma. These include compensation for physical pain and suffering, ongoing mental anguish, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), physical disfigurement, and loss of enjoyment of life activities. In catastrophic claims involving married victims, spouses may independently pursue loss of consortium claims to address the disruption of marital intimacy, companionship, and household support.

Punitive Damages

Unlike compensatory damages, punitive damages are not designed to repay losses. Instead, they serve to punish gross negligence and deter future reckless corporate conduct. To secure punitive damages, the attorney must prove that the defendant acted with conscious disregard for human safety. Examples include a motor carrier knowingly dispatching a driver who has exceeded driving hours for three consecutive days or altering maintenance logs to hide failed air brakes.

Comparison: Commercial Truck Accident vs Passenger Car Claims

Litigation Element Standard Passenger Car Accident Commercial Truck Accident
Governing Regulatory Framework State motor vehicle traffic codes only. Title 49 CFR federal safety statutes, DOT rules, and state commercial codes.
Potential Liable Parties Generally individual at-fault driver only. Driver, motor carrier, freight broker, cargo loaders, maintenance shops, and manufacturers.
Mandatory Insurance Minimums State minimums typically $15,000 to $50,000. Federal minimums of $750,000 to $5,000,000 plus commercial excess syndicates.
Primary Evidence Sources Police crash reports, eyewitness statements, and cell photos. ECM black box data, ELD records, telematics, DVIR reports, and maintenance logs.
Defense Response Speed Standard claims adjuster assigned within days. Corporate rapid-response legal and accident reconstruction teams on scene within hours.

Step-by-Step Roadmap of a Commercial Truck Accident Claim

Commercial trucking litigation follows a structured, multi-phase progression. Understanding each phase helps injured victims set realistic expectations regarding timelines and necessary legal procedures.

Litigation Phase Estimated Timeline Core Strategic Actions Taken by Counsel
1. Emergency Investigation & Evidence Lockdown Days 1 to 30 Issue formal spoliation notices, download ECM black box data, inspect tractor and trailer, obtain police crash reports.
2. Medical Recovery & Damage Formulations Months 1 to 6 Monitor maximum medical improvement (MMI), retain medical experts, calculate future earnings loss, draft comprehensive life care plans.
3. Pre-Suit Settlement Demand & Negotiations Months 6 to 9 Prepare extensive demand package detailing liability, regulatory violations, and financial losses; negotiate with insurers.
4. Formal Lawsuit Filing & Discovery Phase Months 9 to 18 File summons and complaint, subpoena internal dispatch files, depose safety directors and drivers, exchange expert witness reports.
5. Alternative Dispute Resolution or Jury Trial Months 18 to 24+ Participate in formal mediation or judicial arbitration; present forensic exhibits and witness testimony at civil jury trial if unpaid.

Understanding Legal Fees: The Contingency Arrangement

A common concern among injured motorists is the financial cost of retaining elite legal representation against well-funded trucking conglomerates. Fortunately, personal injury and commercial truck accident attorneys operate almost universally on a contingency fee basis.

Under a standard contingency fee agreement, the client pays zero upfront legal fees and zero out-of-pocket hourly rates. The attorney legal compensation is tied directly to the successful outcome of the case. The attorney receives an agreed-upon percentage, typically between 33.3% and 40% depending on whether the dispute resolves before or after filing a formal lawsuit, taken directly from the final settlement or court judgment.

Furthermore, established commercial truck injury law firms advance all necessary litigation expenses throughout the duration of the claim. These advanced costs cover independent accident reconstruction engineers, digital forensic extraction fees, expert medical testimony, deposition transcripts, and court filing fees. If the legal team fails to secure a financial recovery, the client owes nothing in attorney fees. This structure ensures that injured individuals have equal access to justice, regardless of their immediate financial circumstances.

Essential Actions to Take Immediately Following a Truck Wreck

The steps taken in the hours and days following a commercial truck collision substantially impact both medical outcomes and the strength of subsequent legal claims. Following an established protocol protects your health and preserves vital legal rights.

  • Call Emergency Services Immediately: Request police, emergency medical technicians, and fire personnel. Ensure law enforcement creates a formal State Police or Highway Patrol Commercial Vehicle Collision Report.
  • Seek Immediate Emergency Medical Treatment: Adrenaline masks severe trauma. Undergo comprehensive trauma screening, CT scans, and neurological evaluations even if you feel capable of walking away from the scene.
  • Document the Scene Safely: If physically able without risking injury, capture photos and video of all vehicles, skid marks, road conditions, debris fields, and DOT identification numbers printed on the truck door.
  • Obtain Critical Identification Data: Record the truck driver name, CDL number, commercial insurance policy details, motor carrier name, and USDOT operating number. Collect contact information from independent eyewitnesses.
  • Do Not Provide Recorded Statements: Commercial insurance adjusters will contact you quickly seeking recorded interviews. Decline politely, stating that you will communicate through legal counsel.
  • Retain a Qualified Truck Accident Attorney: Engage specialized legal representation immediately so an emergency spoliation notice can be issued before critical ECM black box data is lost.

Frequently Asked Questions About Truck Accident Lawsuits

How long do I have to file a truck accident lawsuit?

The legal deadline to file a personal injury lawsuit, known as the statute of limitations, varies by state, typically ranging between one and three years from the date of the collision. However, waiting to contact an attorney is dangerous because critical electronic black box evidence can be overwritten in as little as 30 days.

What if the trucking company claims the driver is an independent contractor?

Trucking companies frequently classify drivers as independent contractors to escape liability. However, under federal motor carrier regulations, if the truck displays the carrier USDOT number or operates under its federal operating authority, the carrier can still be held legally responsible for the driver actions during commercial freight transport.

How much is a commercial truck accident settlement worth?

Settlement values depend on several factors, including the severity of injuries, total medical expenses, permanent impairment ratings, lost earnings capacity, and available commercial insurance policy limits. Because commercial trucks carry policies starting at $750,000 up to several million dollars, catastrophic claims often resolve for substantial sums when liability is clearly proven.

What is an ECM black box and why is it so important?

The Electronic Control Module (ECM) is an onboard computer that records vehicle operational data during a crash. It captures speed, braking timing, throttle position, and engine performance seconds before impact, providing objective proof of driver speed or failure to brake that refutes false driver testimony.

Can multiple parties be held liable for my truck accident?

Yes. Commercial transportation claims frequently involve joint liability among multiple entities, including the truck driver, the motor carrier company, third-party cargo loading warehouses, freight logistics brokers, and maintenance contractors. Identifying all liable parties increases the total insurance coverage available for recovery.

Do I have to pay upfront fees to hire a truck accident attorney?

No. Reputable personal injury attorneys work on a contingency fee basis. You pay no retainer or hourly fees out of pocket. Legal fees and litigation costs are deducted as an agreed percentage only after your legal team successfully secures a financial settlement or trial award.
