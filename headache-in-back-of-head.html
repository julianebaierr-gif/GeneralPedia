Headache in Back of Head: Causes, Types & Doctor Care

Discover what causes a headache in the back of your head. Explore tension, occipital neuralgia, neck strain, and critical red flags.

Elena Torres
Recent
15 min read
Clinical & Medical Notice: This guide provides evidence-based educational information on posterior cranial pain, cervical spine mechanics, and neurological headache disorders. It is not a substitute for professional medical diagnosis or clinical treatment. If you experience sudden, severe head pain or neurological changes, seek immediate emergency medical care.

Experiencing a persistent headache in back of head is one of the most frequent reasons patients consult primary care physicians and neurologists. When individuals wonder why would my head hurt or why is my head hurting so bad, discomfort centered at the skull base, upper neck, or radiating up over the scalp can feel alarming, disrupting concentration, sleep, and physical comfort. While the vast majority of posterior head pain cases stem from manageable musculoskeletal and nerve conditions, understanding the exact root cause is vital for choosing the right recovery path.

Posterior cranial pain rarely acts as an isolated condition. Many people struggle to describe the sensation, wondering what does back head pain mean or what does back of head headache mean when they feel a headache pain behind head, headache across back of head, or headache rear of head. In clinical neurology, this symptom serves as a physiological signal generated where the cervical spine, suboccipital muscle groups, and sensitive sensory nerves meet. This comprehensive medical guide examines what causes your head to hurt in the back, how to distinguish between tension headaches, occipital neuralgia, and cervical spine disorders, why pain radiates to the eyes and ears, and doctor-approved recovery protocols.

The Three Most Common Causes of Back-of-Head Pain

When patients arrive at a clinic asking why does the back of my head hurt, why is the back of my head hurting, or what causes headaches in the back of your head, neurologists categorize the presentation into three primary conditions:

1. Tension-Type Headaches (Musculoskeletal Strain)

A tension headache at base of skull represents the single most common headache diagnosis worldwide. When physical fatigue, emotional stress, or sustained posture causes the suboccipital muscles and upper trapezius to remain contracted, pain radiates upward. This creates a dull pain at base of skull, dull ache base of skull, or general pressure at base of skull.

  • Pain Quality: A dull, non-throbbing pressure resembling a tight band or heavy vice encircling the cranial vault.
  • Distribution: Typically bilateral, presenting as a headache that starts at base of skull and spreads around both temples.
  • Physical Signs: Palpable muscle pain at base of skull without nausea or photophobia.

2. Occipital Neuralgia (Nerve Compression and Irritation)

When individuals ask what causes sharp pains in your head or why am i getting sharp pains in my head, the diagnosis frequently points to occipital neuralgia. This occurs when the greater or lesser occipital nerves (which emerge between the C2 and C3 cervical vertebrae and travel upward through the scalp) become compressed or inflamed.

  • Pain Quality: Intense, piercing, burning, or electric shock sensations that shoot from the skull base toward the crown.
  • Distribution: Often unilateral, causing back left side of head pain or ache back of head right side, though both nerves can be involved.
  • Distinctive Marker: Significant scalp tenderness, where resting on a pillow or brushing hair triggers sharp discomfort.

3. Cervicogenic Headaches (Referred Cervical Spine Pain)

When people notice that neck and back of head hurt simultaneously, or ask why does my head hurt when i move my neck, a cervicogenic headache is often the underlying issue. Unlike primary headaches, this is a secondary condition caused by cervical disc herniation, osteoarthritis, or facet joint inflammation in the C1 to C3 vertebrae.

  • Pain Quality: A steady, deep ache originating in the cervical spine and referring upward toward the occiput and forehead.
  • Distribution: Strictly side-locked, causing pain on right back side or head hurts left side back.
  • Mechanical Trigger: Rotating the neck, looking upward, or holding static angles reliably provokes or intensifies the episode.

Pain and Tenderness at the Base of the Skull

A common reason patients seek clinical care is pain in back of head at base of skull or severe neck pain and headache base of skull. Individuals frequently ask why is the base of my skull sore or why does the base of my skull and neck hurt. This anatomical region, known as the suboccipital junction, contains a dense network of muscles, ligaments, and cervical nerves.

When examining what causes headaches at the base of the skull, clinical evaluations often reveal neck strain base of skull. Patients frequently report that the base of skull tender to touch, experiencing noticeable base of skull neck soreness or persistent soreness at base of skull. In many cases, the base of skull hurts when pressed or feels base of skull sore to touch, indicating active myofascial trigger points in the rectus capitis and obliquus capitis muscles.

Depending on posture and ergonomics, discomfort may present as a bad headache at base of skull, intense pressure in back of head at base of skull, or a localized headache at base of skull. Patients often wonder why does the back right side of my head hurt, why does the lower back of my head hurt, or why does the left back of my head hurt. Asymmetry is common: a headache on lower left side of head or neck pain left side base of skull often stems from sleeping on an unsupportive pillow or tilting the head toward one side during phone calls. Conversely, headache lower back right side of head can result from mouse-hand postural strain pulling on the right trapezius.

Why Headaches Radiate Behind the Eyes and Temples

Patients often arrive at neurology clinics confused by widespread facial symptoms, asking why do i have a headache behind my eyes, why do i have a headache behind my right eye, or what links headache and eye pain with posterior neck stiffness.

The anatomical answer lies in the trigeminocervical complex. Sensory nerve fibers from the upper three cervical nerve roots (C1, C2, and C3) converge with sensory fibers from the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal cranial nerve inside the upper spinal cord. Because these pathways share common second-order neurons, the brain often misinterprets pain originating at the base of the neck as coming from the front of the head.

This explains why a person with occipital nerve irritation can develop a headache behind eyes, headache behind right eye, or headache on top of head. Furthermore, patients frequently ask why does the right side of my head hurt, why is the right side of my head hurting, or why does my head hurt in one spot. When cervical facet joint irritation is concentrated on the right side, it produces consistent headache pain on the right side that arches over the crown and settles directly behind the eye socket.

Headaches Behind the Ear, Earaches, and Neck Tension

Another frequent presentation involves concurrent head and ear symptoms, leading patients to ask why does my head hurt behind my ear or why does my ear and head hurt. Complaints such as cranial pain behind ear, headache back of ear, sharp pain behind ear, and headache behind ear are closely tied to regional cervical anatomy.

The lesser occipital nerve and greater auricular nerve travel directly across the sternocleidomastoid muscle and mastoid process, located immediately behind the earlobe. When neck muscles spasm, patients frequently experience stiff neck headache and ear pain, soreness in neck under ear, or pain on side of neck under ear. This can feel like pain inside ear and head, prompting questions like can ear pain cause a headache or can an ear ache cause a headache.

Patients often report one-sided patterns, such as earache and headache on left side, right ear hurts and headache, neck ear pain one side, neck and ear pain on left side, or earache and headache right side. When someone suffers from neck ache headache earache, neck and ear ache, or general head and ear ache, doctors must differentiate between cervical spine referral and true otologic pathology:

  • Eustachian Tube and Middle Ear Issues: When ear infection head pain, fluid buildup, or middle ear inflammation occurs, patients wonder can fluid in the ears cause headaches or can a blocked ear cause a headache. Increased middle ear pressure irritates the tympanic plexus, causing headache and ear pressure, headache and sore ears, or ear ache neck ache.
  • Myofascial and Temporomandibular Strain: Clenching the jaw or grinding teeth strains the pterygoid and masseter muscles, causing causes of headache and ear pain where neck hurts ear infection symptoms are mimicked without true infection.
  • Referred Cervical Tension: When earache with headache and neck pain or ear pain leads to headache occurs without fever or ear canal redness, the issue almost always stems from upper cervical facet strain referring upward through the greater auricular pathway.

Can Back Pain and Lower Back Strain Cause Headaches?

Many individuals experience full-spine discomfort and wonder can back pain cause headaches, does backache cause headaches, or can lower back pain cause headaches. Symptoms such as throbbing headache and lower back pain, back ache causing headache, and lower back pain headache fatigue are widely documented in clinical spine practices.

Understanding how back ache and headache or lower back pain and headache connect requires looking at the full spinal kinetic chain. The human spine functions as a continuous biomechanical unit. When lumbar disc degeneration, poor lumbar support, or pelvic tilt creates lower back strain, the thoracic spine compensates by increasing its curvature (kyphosis). To keep the eyes level with the horizon, the cervical spine must hyperextend, placing immense mechanical stress on the suboccipital muscles and causing back pain and back head pain.

Furthermore, the dura mater (the tough protective membrane encasing the spinal cord) anchors at the sacrum and travels uninterrupted up the spinal canal to attach firmly around the foramen magnum at the skull base. Physical tension in the lower spine can create longitudinal dural traction, contributing to back pain migraine headaches and persistent posterior head stiffness.

Modern Tech Neck and Biomechanical Triggers

In modern ergonomic evaluations, poor postural habits have emerged as the leading driver of posterior cranial pain among office professionals, remote workers, and smartphone users. This phenomenon, clinically recognized as forward head posture or tech neck, creates severe biomechanical imbalance that explains why does the back of my neck and head hurt so frequently.

In an anatomically neutral position, an adult human head weighs approximately 10 to 12 pounds. However, when you tilt your head forward at a 45-degree angle to review a laptop screen or text on a mobile phone, the mechanical gravitational force on the cervical spine increases to approximately 49 pounds. At a 60-degree angle, that load reaches an astonishing 60 pounds.

To prevent your head from falling forward under this weight, the deep suboccipital muscles must remain in state of sustained isometric contraction. Over weeks and months, these muscle groups shorten, develop fibrotic myofascial trigger points, and physically compress the greater occipital nerve where it pierces the muscular sheath, producing relentless posterior headaches.

Other Medical Causes, Blood Pressure, and Rebound Pain

Beyond primary muscular and nerve compression, several physiological factors can trigger headaches localized to the back of the skull:

  • Medication-Overuse (Rebound) Headaches: Taking over-the-counter pain relievers more than two to three days per week can lead to chronic daily rebound headaches that regularly concentrate at the base of the head as each dose wears off.
  • Severe Hypertensive Crisis: While mild hypertension does not produce head pain, a severe spike in blood pressure (systolic above 180 mmHg or diastolic above 120 mmHg) can cause a dangerous, pounding posterior headache. If accompanied by blurred vision, chest tightness, or dizziness, seek emergency care immediately.
  • Exertional and Cough Headaches: Strenuous physical exertion, weightlifting, intense coughing, or straining increases intrathoracic and intracranial pressure, precipitating sudden bursts of occipital pain. While often benign, primary exertional headaches require physician evaluation to exclude structural abnormalities like Chiari malformations.
  • Spontaneous Intracranial Hypotension (Low CSF Pressure): A tear in the spinal dura can allow cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to leak, lowering intracranial pressure. This condition produces a classic positional headache that worsens significantly when sitting or standing and resolves quickly after lying flat.

Evidence-Based At-Home Relief and Neck Stretches

For benign tension and posture-related posterior headaches, practicing targeted physical therapy techniques provides rapid, sustainable muscular decompression:

  1. Chin Tucks (Cervical Retraction): Sit upright in an ergonomic chair with your shoulders relaxed. Looking straight ahead, gently draw your chin straight back, as though making a gentle double chin, without tilting your head up or down. Hold for 5 seconds and release. Repeat 10 times. This movement strengthens deep neck flexors and decompresses suboccipital nerve roots.
  2. Suboccipital Tennis Ball Release: Place two tennis balls inside a sock and tie it off tightly. Lie flat on your back on a firm surface, positioning the balls directly beneath the base of your skull on either side of the spine. Relax your breathing and allow the weight of your head to apply gentle pressure for 5 to 10 minutes, releasing tense muscular trigger points.
  3. Targeted Temperature Protocol: Apply an ice pack wrapped in a cloth to the base of the skull for 15 minutes to reduce acute nerve inflammation during occipital neuralgia flares. Alternatively, use a moist heating pad across the shoulders and neck to relax rigid muscle spasms caused by tension headaches.
  4. Workstation Ergonomic Corrections: Position your primary computer monitor so that the top third of the screen sits directly at eye level, roughly an arm length away. Keep your feet flat on the floor, utilize an ergonomic chair with lumbar support, and take a 60-second posture reset break every 30 minutes.

Medical Diagnostics, Nerve Blocks, and Clinical Therapies

When conservative home care does not resolve chronic back-of-head pain, specialized clinical interventions provide targeted relief under physician supervision:

Diagnostic Imaging and Nerve Palpation: A neurologist or spine specialist conducts physical palpation over the greater occipital nerve exit site (reproducing symptoms via Tinel's sign) and evaluates cervical range of motion. Cervical spine X-rays or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and cervical spine help identify disc herniation, facet arthropathy, or nerve impingement.

Occipital Nerve Blocks: For patients suffering from confirmed occipital neuralgia, an outpatient greater occipital nerve block delivers remarkable therapeutic results. A specialist injects a localized mixture of local anesthetic (such as bupivacaine) and an anti-inflammatory corticosteroid directly around the nerve bundle. Pain relief often begins within minutes and can persist for weeks or months.

Prescription Pharmacotherapy: Doctors may recommend neuropathic medications such as gabapentin or pregabalin to calm hyperactive sensory nerves, short-term muscle relaxants (like tizanidine or cyclobenzaprine) for severe spasms, or low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (such as amitriptyline) to prevent chronic tension headache pathways.

Interventional and Physical Therapies: For refractory cases, long-term options include radiofrequency ablation (RFA) to desensitize problematic cervical facet nerves, specialized manual physical therapy, or botulinum toxin (Botox) injections designed to relax chronic muscular contraction patterns across the upper cervical chain.

Frequently Asked Questions About Back of Head Headaches

What causes a headache in the back of the head?

The most common causes of a headache in the back of the head are tension headaches from muscle contraction, occipital neuralgia from pinched or inflamed scalp nerves, and cervicogenic headaches originating from cervical spine joints or discs. Less common causes include poor posture, migraines, exertional strain, or underlying vascular issues.

How can you tell if a headache is occipital neuralgia or a tension headache?

A tension headache causes a dull, steady, band-like tightness or aching pressure across both sides of the head and neck. In contrast, occipital neuralgia produces sharp, stabbing, burning, or electric shock-like jolts of pain that travel upward from the base of the skull along one or both sides of the scalp, often making the scalp tender to light touch.

Why does the back of my head hurt when I turn my neck?

Pain that worsens with neck movement or holding your head in awkward postures typically points to a cervicogenic headache or cervical spine facet joint irritation. When you rotate or tilt your neck, damaged cervical discs or inflamed suboccipital muscles compress local nerve pathways, sending referred pain directly into the base of the skull.

Can poor posture and computer screen use cause headaches at the base of the skull?

Yes. Forward head posture, commonly called tech neck, forces the suboccipital muscles at the base of your skull to work under excessive mechanical strain. For every inch your head juts forward, your neck muscles must support an extra 10 pounds of effective load, leading to chronic muscle spasms that compress the greater occipital nerve.

Is a sharp shooting pain in the back of the head dangerous?

Brief, electric jolts that last a few seconds are usually caused by benign nerve irritation such as occipital neuralgia or ice-pick headaches. However, a sudden, blinding headache that peaks in intensity within 60 seconds (thunderclap headache) or is accompanied by neurological symptoms like vision changes or numbness requires immediate emergency medical evaluation.

Can high blood pressure cause pain in the back of the head?

Moderate high blood pressure rarely causes headaches. However, a hypertensive crisis (blood pressure exceeding 180/120 mmHg) can trigger a severe, throbbing headache typically localized to the back of the head or nape of the neck, often accompanied by blurred vision, chest pain, dizziness, or shortness of breath.

What is the fastest way to relieve a headache in the back of the head at home?

Fast relief strategies include applying an ice pack to the base of the skull for sharp nerve pain, using moist heat for tight muscle spasms, performing gentle chin tucks to decompress cervical nerves, taking over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medications like ibuprofen, and resting in a dark, quiet room with proper cervical neck support.

When should you go to the emergency room for pain in the back of the head?

Seek emergency care immediately if you experience a thunderclap headache (worst headache of your life peaking in seconds), pain accompanied by high fever and a stiff neck, sudden weakness, numbness, speech difficulty, confusion, or severe neck and head pain following head trauma or sudden neck manipulation.

Does sleeping on the wrong pillow cause back of head headaches?

Yes. Pillows that are too thick, too flat, or unsupportive force the cervical spine into unnatural flexion, extension, or lateral rotation for several hours. This sustained awkward angle strains the upper trapezius and suboccipital muscles, causing you to wake up with posterior neck stiffness and a dull occipital headache.

What doctor should I see for persistent back of head pain?

Start with a primary care physician who can conduct a physical examination and rule out systemic issues. If symptoms persist, your doctor may refer you to a neurologist for specialized headache evaluation, an orthopedic spine specialist, or a licensed physical therapist who specializes in cervical spine rehabilitation.
